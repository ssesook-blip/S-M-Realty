Modular Homes · Dominican Republic

Modular homes for the North Coast, built in a factory and set on your lot

A finished home that arrives by sea, sits on a simple foundation and can be lived in within weeks of landing. Here's exactly what these units are made of, what they cost, and what it really takes to put one on a lot in Sosúa, Cabarete or Puerto Plata.

By Sheena Sesook & Michael Galea · November 2026

The short version

  • Two sizes: a 20 ft studio (13 m², sleeps 1 to 2) and a 40 ft two-bedroom (26 m², sleeps 2 to 3). Units can be stacked or joined for larger layouts.
  • A galvanized steel frame, metal exterior panels, rock-wool insulation and double-glazed tempered glass, fitted out with kitchen, bathroom, beds, lighting and wiring before it ships.
  • Indicative pricing from about US$16,900 (20 ft) and US$29,500 (40 ft), shipped to Caucedo port. Final price is confirmed per order.
  • Built in about 35 working days after deposit, then shipped by sea to the Dominican Republic.
  • You still need land, permits, a foundation and utility hookups. We help with all of that, and with finding the right lot.

Is this "container living"?

Not quite, and the difference matters. A shipping-container home is a used or new steel cargo box with windows cut into it. The walls are corrugated steel, so heat and condensation are a constant fight in the Caribbean, and every opening weakens the box.

These units are built the other way around. Each one starts as a purpose-made frame of galvanized steel tube, then gets insulated walls and roof, glass walls, interior finishes and full plumbing and electrical, all in the factory. They're sized to ship by standard container freight, which is why people call them container homes, but structurally and comfort-wise they're closer to a small finished house. Building with one in the Caribbean: what to plan for

A modular home is a good fit for the North Coast, but only if it's set up for the climate. These are the points we'd go through with you before ordering.

Hurricanes and anchoring

The steel frame is strong and light, which is exactly why anchoring matters. Each unit has to be bolted down to a proper foundation (concrete piers or a slab) designed by a local engineer for wind loads in your exact spot. The glass is tempered double glazing, which is much stronger than ordinary glass but is not laminated impact glass. On an exposed or beachfront lot, plan for hurricane shutters or ask for an impact-glass upgrade.

Earthquakes

The Dominican Republic is also a seismic zone. A welded steel frame flexes rather than cracks, which is a real advantage over unreinforced block. The foundation connection is still the critical part, so it should be engineered rather than improvised.

Salt air

Near the sea, everything metal corrodes faster. The galvanized frame and aluminium windows are the right starting point. Rinse the exterior and hardware with fresh water from time to time, and check fixings and sealant joints once a year.

Heat

Lots of glass means lots of light and also lots of sun. Face the glass wall away from the afternoon sun where you can, use the deck or a pergola for shade, and plan on a split air conditioner (the 20 ft plan already shows its position). The foil-faced rock wool and insulated glass do most of the work after that.

Power and water

Dominican power is 110 to 120 V at 60 Hz, the same as North America, and the outlets are specified for that. Confirm the voltage and frequency on the order so the whole system matches. Plan for a water connection or cistern and pump, and a septic system if the lot isn't on a sewer line, as with any home here.

Permits and community rules

A modular home is still a home in the eyes of the municipality. The land title needs to be clean and the build needs the usual approvals before it goes in. Many gated communities on the North Coast also have their own architectural rules, and some don't allow modular or prefabricated homes at all. Check this before you buy a lot, not after. It's one of the first things we confirm for you.

The land is the part most people underestimate. A lot that allows modular construction, has road access wide enough for a truck and crane, and already has power and water nearby will save you far more than any difference in unit price. That's where we come in.

Frequently asked questions

Are these shipping containers converted into homes?

No. They're purpose-built modules on a galvanized steel tube frame, with insulated walls and roof, glass walls and full interior finishes. They're sized to ship with standard container freight, which is why they're often called container homes.

How big are they?

The 20 ft studio is 5.80 × 2.25 m outside (13 m² of floor area) and sleeps 1 to 2. The 40 ft model is 11.50 × 2.25 m (26 m²) with two bedrooms and sleeps 2 to 3. Both are 2.45 m tall outside. Modules can be stacked or joined for larger homes, like the two-storey units pictured.

Can they handle hurricanes?

The steel frame is strong, but storm performance depends on how the unit is anchored to its foundation and on protecting the glass. Have a local engineer design the foundation and tie-downs for your site, and add hurricane shutters or impact glass on exposed lots.

Will the electrical system work in the Dominican Republic?

Yes. The outlets are specified in the American flat-pin style used in the DR. Confirm 110 to 120 V and 60 Hz on the order so everything matches the local grid.

Can I put one in a gated community?

It depends on the community. Some allow modular homes and some don't, and most have design rules. We check the rules for any lot before you commit.

How long does it take?

About 35 working days to build after the deposit, then ocean shipping, customs and trucking to the North Coast. Site preparation can run at the same time.

Do you have land for one?

Yes. We list lots across Sosúa, Cabarete and Puerto Plata, and we can shortlist ones where modular construction is allowed and practical. Message us with your budget and what you'd like to build.
